# Lesson 2: Fundamental Mechanical Principles of Vehicle Driving
## Learning Objectives of This Lesson
- *Understand all forces involved in vehicle acceleration, deceleration and steering;*
- *Distinguish static friction / sliding friction and grasp the essence of tyre slip;*
- *Build basic concepts of torque, horsepower and rotational speed (no complex formulas);*
- *Master the underlying causes of weight transfer, body roll during cornering, understeer and oversteer;*
- *Link to subsequent content: design logic of suspension, tyres, differential and transmission.*

## I. Five Major Resistances to Vehicle Motion
***To move forward, the driving force output by the powertrain must overcome the sum of all resistances:***
- **Rolling Resistance**:`Generated by continuous deformation of compressed tyres and micro-friction between rubber and the ground. Lower tyre pressure, softer tyres and heavier load lead to higher rolling resistance.
At low speeds, rolling resistance is the dominant resistance; its proportion drops at high speeds.`
> Extension: Comfort tyres with higher aspect ratios have greater rolling resistance than low-aspect-ratio performance tyres.

- **Aerodynamic Drag**:`Formed by airflow hitting the front fascia, airflow separation around the vehicle body and negative pressure drag at the rear. Aerodynamic drag is proportional to the square of vehicle speed. If speed doubles, aerodynamic drag quadruples. This explains why additional acceleration at high speeds consumes much more fuel or electricity, and also accounts for the extensive aerodynamic kits fitted on sports cars.`

- **Gradient Resistance**:`Additional resistance against gravity component when climbing uphill; gradient resistance reverses downhill and pushes the vehicle to coast.`
- **Acceleration Inertia Resistance**:`Objects tend to maintain their original motion state. During launch and hard acceleration, extra power is required to overcome vehicle inertia; releasing the throttle lets inertia keep the vehicle moving forward.`
- **Mechanical Resistance**:`Internal engine friction, meshing friction of transmission gears, bearing resistance and half-shaft resistance. Engine oil condition, gear oil viscosity and bearing wear directly affect the magnitude of mechanical resistance.`

> Simplified power balance summary:
>- **Driving Force ≥ Rolling Resistance + Aerodynamic Drag + Gradient Resistance + Acceleration Resistance → Vehicle accelerates or maintains constant speed**
>- **Driving Force ＜ Total Resistance → Vehicle decelerates**

## II. Driving Force Originates from Tyre Friction *(Core Focus)*
***The rotating tyre tread attempts to slide backward relative to the ground. The ground exerts a forward static frictional force on the tyre, and this friction is the genuine driving force propelling the whole vehicle.***

### Static Friction vs Sliding Friction
1. **Static Friction (Normal Driving)**
`No relative slip exists between tread and ground, delivering maximum grip.`
2. **Sliding Friction (Tyre Slip)**
`Excessive engine torque causes tyre overspeed and continuous wheel spin. Sliding friction is far weaker than maximum static friction.
Typical phenomena: tyre smoking during launch, loss of control on wet or icy roads, front wheel spin under hard acceleration for high-power front-wheel-drive vehicles.`

> Key Conclusion:
No matter how much horsepower the engine produces, **the upper limit of driving force = maximum static friction of tyres**.
This is the fundamental reason high-performance vehicles adopt wider tyres to boost grip.

## III. Popular Explanation of Torque, Horsepower and RPM (Top Priority)
### 1. Torque *(N·m)*
**Simple definition: rotational "twisting force"**

It represents the instantaneous twisting force output by the crankshaft or drive shaft.

Vehicles that generate high torque at low RPM: `easier launch, climbing and heavy-load operation (typical characteristics of turbocharged vehicles and electric motors).`

### 2. Horsepower *(Power)*
***Horsepower measures the rate of work and continuous acceleration capability.***
> Logical formula: `Horsepower is determined jointly by torque and rotational speed.
With fixed torque, higher rotational speed brings higher horsepower.`

> Visual analogy:
>- Torque = the maximum weight you can push in one single movement
>- Horsepower = how fast you can continuously push heavy objects

- Electric motors: `Deliver peak torque at zero rotational speed with strong low-end burst performance;`
- Naturally aspirated engines: `Torque rises gradually with rotational speed;`
- Turbocharged engines: `Torque surges rapidly once boost threshold RPM is reached.`

### 3. Extended Knowledge (Foundation for Transmission Lessons)
*Engines have limited native torque. Sets of large and small gears inside the transmission function to **amplify torque**, and lower gears offer a higher torque amplification ratio.*

## IV. Braking Forces and Weight Transfer
> When the vehicle brakes, inertia shifts vehicle weight forward:
>- Vertical load on front wheels rises → front wheel grip increases;
>- Vertical load on rear wheels drops → rear wheel grip decreases.
Phenomenon: Vehicle nose dives under braking.

***Conversely: Hard acceleration transfers weight rearward, compressing the rear of the vehicle.***
- Front-engine front-wheel-drive (FF) hard acceleration:`Reduced front wheel load easily triggers wheel slip and weakens steering capability;`
- Front-engine rear-wheel-drive (FR) hard acceleration:`Increased rear wheel load improves launch grip, which is the core mechanical reason rear-wheel-drive layouts suit high-power setups.`

## V. Steering, Centrifugal Force and Body Roll
***Centrifugal force emerges when the vehicle corners; inertia attempts to keep the vehicle travelling in a straight line.
Centrifugal force positively correlates with vehicle speed, and smaller turning radius creates stronger centrifugal force.***

1. **Principle of Body Roll**
`Centrifugal force acts on the vehicle centre of mass. Since the centre of mass sits higher than suspension pivot points, a moment is formed, compressing the outer suspension and stretching the inner suspension, tilting the vehicle toward the outside of the bend.
Body roll is not inherently a flaw. Suspension bushings, dampers and springs are tuned to balance roll amplitude; excessive roll alters tyre contact angles and reduces grip.`

> Two fundamental steering characteristics
>- **Understeer**: Front wheels reach the grip limit first, making the vehicle tend to drift outwards from the bend. Most factory-tuned front-wheel-drive passenger cars feature mild understeer for better safety;
>- **Oversteer**: Rear wheels lose grip first, causing the rear end to slide outward. Common in aggressive rear-wheel-drive driving with higher handling potential but greater driving difficulty.
